During the early 2010s, the country entered a period of rapid economic
expansion driven by low interest rates, rising consumer confidence, and a
wave of technological breakthroughs. Venture funding tripled in five
years, and new firms emerged faster than regulators could track them.
Policymakers celebrated the momentum and encouraged even more aggressive
growth, framing the expansion as a historic opportunity that should not
be slowed down by “unnecessary caution.” 

By the mid-2010s, signs of overheating became visible. Real estate prices
surged to record highs, personal debt climbed steadily, and several
sectors began relying on speculative investment rather than actual
productivity gains. Analysts warned that the pace of expansion was
outstripping the economy’s structural capacity, but these warnings were
publicly dismissed as “pessimistic noise.” Opportunity narratives
dominated, even as underlying vulnerabilities accumulated.

The cycle broke abruptly when a sudden downturn in global demand and a
cascade of loan defaults triggered a nationwide financial shock. Within
weeks, credit markets froze and major institutions required emergency
intervention. The government responded with sweeping stabilization
measures: strict capital controls, mandatory liquidity buffers, a freeze
on high-risk lending, and emergency taxation policies aimed at preserving
basic system integrity. The expansion logic evaporated almost overnight.

Even after markets had largely stabilized, authorities kept the emergency
regime in place. New data showed that risk levels had fallen sharply, yet
the regulatory apparatus remained locked in high-alert mode. Officials
argued that “the lessons of the crisis must not be forgotten” and that
easing restrictions too quickly could trigger a second collapse. Many
business associations complained that the continued restrictions were
suppressing recovery and discouraging innovation, but ministries insisted
that caution was still justified.

As the economy slowly regained momentum, the system moved into a mixed
mode: regulators experimented with selective relaxations, while industry
leaders pushed for broader liberalization. Some sectors experienced a mild
renewed expansion as innovation returned, while others remained constrained
by lingering crisis-era controls. Neither stabilizing nor expansionary
logic fully dominated, producing a patchwork of cautious growth and
persistent restrictions.

The full cycle illustrates how economic systems transition among OS_E
expansion, OS_E overshoot, OS_L stabilization, OS_L hysteresis, and
eventual rebalancing into a mixed operational mode.
