The government’s annual economic report paints a contradictory picture.
On the one hand, the country is experiencing its strongest period of
technological innovation in over a decade: startup formation is rising,
foreign investment has reached a new peak, and several breakthrough
technologies are moving from research labs into commercial deployment.

At the same time, security agencies warn of increasing cyber intrusions,
financial instability in the banking sector, and an uptick in foreign
influence operations targeting critical infrastructure. Multiple agencies
have recommended immediate tightening of digital regulations and stricter
licensing requirements for high-risk technologies.

Economic advisors, however, argue that imposing new constraints right now
would jeopardize the fragile momentum in the innovation sector. Investors
are already cautious, and any signal of regulatory hardening might push
emerging companies to relocate abroad. “We cannot afford to suffocate our
own breakthroughs,” one advisor notes.

Meanwhile, national security officials insist that failure to act quickly
will leave the country vulnerable to cascading risks that could undermine
economic gains entirely. “A single coordinated attack could erase years of
progress,” warns the intelligence briefing.

The cabinet therefore finds itself caught between two imperatives:
protecting national stability and accelerating technological expansion.
With compelling arguments on both sides, no clear consensus emerges.
Policymaking stalls, and the government effectively enters a mixed
operational mode in which both expansionary and stabilizing logics are
active but partially cancel each other out.

This tension illustrates how competing high-intensity signals can generate
a hybrid state that resists straightforward classification into either
OS_L or OS_E.
